The Integumentary System

The integumentary system is the body's largest system. It includes the cutaneous membrane, which is the skin, plus accessory structures such as hair, hair follicles, exocrine glands, and nails.

Must-know points

  • The system makes up about 16% of body weight and covers about 1.5 to 2 square meters.
  • The skin has two major layers: the epidermis and dermis.
  • Accessory structures originate in the dermis and extend to the surface.
  • Major functions include protection, excretion, temperature regulation, melanin production, keratin production, vitamin D3 synthesis, lipid storage, sensory detection, and immune response coordination.

Cutaneous membrane and accessory structures

  • The epidermis is the superficial epithelial layer.
  • The dermis is the deeper connective tissue layer.
  • Hair, hair follicles, glands, and nails are accessory structures even when they extend to the visible surface.

Protection is more than a simple covering

  • Keratin helps resist abrasion and water loss.
  • Melanin helps protect cells from ultraviolet radiation.
  • Glands help cool the body, excrete wastes, lubricate skin, and inhibit bacterial growth.
  • Immune cells help coordinate responses against pathogens and abnormal cells.
Helpful scaffold: A useful fortress analogy: the epidermis acts like walls and gates, glands and melanocytes help manage threats, the subcutaneous layer stores insulation and energy, and repair cells rebuild damaged areas.

The epidermis is stratified squamous epithelium. Its most abundant cells are keratinocytes, which produce keratin as they mature and migrate outward.

Must-know points

  • Thin skin covers most of the body and has four keratinocyte layers.
  • Thick skin is found on palms and soles and has five layers.
  • The stratum lucidum is present only in thick skin.
  • Keratinization is the maturation and upward movement of keratinocytes toward the surface.

From deep to superficial

  • Stratum basale: deepest layer; anchors to the basement membrane; includes basal cells, melanocytes, and tactile cells.
  • Stratum spinosum: 8 to 10 layers of keratinocytes joined by desmosomes; includes dendritic cells for immune defense.
  • Stratum granulosum: 3 to 5 layers where cells stop dividing and produce keratin and keratohyalin.
  • Stratum lucidum: clear layer found only in thick skin.
  • Stratum corneum: 15 to 30 layers of dead, keratinized, water-resistant cells that shed from the surface.

Specialized epidermal features

  • Basal cells replace lost keratinocytes.
  • Melanocytes produce melanin.
  • Tactile cells detect touch.
  • Epidermal ridges interlock with dermal papillae and help form fingerprints.
  • Dendritic cells help defend against pathogens.

Water loss and EGF

  • Insensible perspiration is unnoticed water evaporation from the skin surface.
  • Sensible perspiration is active sweat gland secretion.
  • Damage to the stratum corneum, such as burns, increases water loss.
  • Epidermal growth factor promotes basal cell division, keratin production, glandular secretion, and epidermal repair.
Helpful scaffold: Top-to-bottom mnemonic: Come Let's Get SunBurned. Corneum, Lucidum, Granulosum, Spinosum, Basale.

The dermis sits between the epidermis and subcutaneous layer. It anchors accessory structures and contains connective tissue, vessels, nerves, glands, and follicles.

Must-know points

  • The papillary layer is areolar tissue with capillaries, lymphatic vessels, sensory neurons, and dermal papillae.
  • The reticular layer is dense irregular connective tissue with collagen and elastic fibers.
  • The subcutaneous layer, or hypodermis, is mostly adipose tissue beneath the dermis.
  • The subcutaneous layer insulates, stores energy, cushions, and helps stabilize skin position.

Dermal strength and movement

  • Collagen fibers resist stretching, add strength, and help prevent damage from excessive movement.
  • Elastic fibers allow stretch and recoil.
  • Skin turgor reflects water content and resilience; dehydration, aging, hormones, and UV exposure can reduce it.
  • Stretch marks occur when excessive stretching damages the dermis.

Tension lines and clinical relevance

  • Tension lines, also called cleavage lines, follow parallel collagen and elastic fiber patterns.
  • Cuts made parallel to tension lines tend to heal faster with less scarring.
  • Cuts made perpendicular to tension lines tend to gap more and scar more.

Blood supply and sensation

  • The cutaneous plexus is an arterial network in the reticular layer.
  • The subpapillary plexus supplies capillary loops in the papillary layer.
  • Bruises, or contusions, occur when damaged blood vessels leak blood into tissues.
  • Tactile corpuscles detect light touch in dermal papillae.
  • Lamellar corpuscles detect deep pressure and vibration in the reticular layer.

Subcutaneous layer

  • The hypodermis connects skin to deeper tissues and organs.
  • It contains large arteries and veins in its superficial region.
  • It is a common site for subcutaneous injections using hypodermic needles.
  • Fat distribution varies with sex hormones; common storage patterns differ between men and women.
Helpful scaffold: Think of the dermis as the flexible but strong support layer under a protective surface.

Skin color is mainly influenced by melanin and carotene. UV exposure also connects skin to vitamin D3 production.

Must-know points

  • Melanin is produced by melanocytes in the stratum basale.
  • Pheomelanin is red-yellow; eumelanin is brown-black.
  • Melanin is packaged in melanosomes and transferred to keratinocytes.
  • Darker skin results from larger and more abundant melanosomes.
  • Carotene is an orange-yellow pigment that can be converted to vitamin A.
  • Vitamin D3 synthesis begins in skin and is completed through liver and kidney processing.

Melanin and UV radiation

  • Melanin absorbs and dissipates ultraviolet radiation.
  • Small amounts of UV radiation help vitamin D3 synthesis.
  • Excessive UV exposure can damage DNA and increase skin cancer risk.
  • A tan is a protective response to UV damage, not a sign that skin is healthier.

Carotene and vitamin A

  • Carotene is found in orange vegetables such as carrots.
  • It can accumulate in epidermal cells, deep dermis, and subcutaneous tissue.
  • Vitamin A helps maintain healthy epithelial tissues and supports photoreceptor pigment synthesis in the eyes.

Vitamin D3 pathway

  • UV radiation converts a steroid precursor in the stratum basale and stratum spinosum into cholecalciferol.
  • The liver converts cholecalciferol into an intermediate product.
  • The kidneys convert the intermediate into calcitriol, the active form.
  • Calcitriol supports calcium and phosphate absorption in the small intestine and helps maintain healthy bones.
  • Insufficient vitamin D3 can lead to rickets in children, especially with limited sunlight or poor dietary intake.
Helpful scaffold: Melanin is like adjustable shade for epidermal cells: helpful against UV, but it also changes how much UV reaches vitamin D precursors.

Accessory structures are derived from the epidermis but are rooted in or associated with the dermis.

Must-know points

  • Hair protects, insulates, guards openings, and enhances sensory perception.
  • Hair is absent from palms, soles, lips, and parts of the external genitalia.
  • Sebaceous glands release sebum that lubricates, protects, inhibits bacteria, and reduces water loss.
  • Sweat glands include apocrine and eccrine glands.
  • Nails are dead, keratinized cells that protect digits, improve grip, and can reflect health conditions.

Hair structure and control

  • The hair shaft is the visible part beyond the skin.
  • The hair root anchors the hair into the skin and may extend into the dermis or subcutaneous layer.
  • The hair follicle surrounds the root, extends deep into the dermis, and is wrapped in dense connective tissue.
  • The root hair plexus is a network of sensory nerves around the follicle.
  • The arrector pili is involuntary smooth muscle; contraction causes goosebumps during cold or emotional states.

Hair types and color

  • Vellus hairs are fine, soft hairs covering most of the body.
  • Terminal hairs are thicker, pigmented hairs found on the scalp, eyebrows, eyelashes, and after puberty in axillary and pubic regions.
  • Hair color depends on the type and amount of melanin produced by melanocytes in the hair matrix.
  • Gray or white hair results from age-related loss of melanocyte activity.

Glands

  • Sebaceous glands are holocrine glands that release sebum into follicles or directly onto skin.
  • Apocrine sweat glands occur in armpits, around nipples, and in the pubic region; they secrete into hair follicles and may become odorous after bacterial activity.
  • Eccrine sweat glands are widespread, especially on palms and soles; they cool the body, excrete water and electrolytes, and help flush microorganisms.
  • Mammary glands are modified sweat glands that produce milk.
  • Ceruminous glands produce earwax in the ear canal.

Nails

  • The nail body is the visible part covering the nail bed.
  • The nail bed provides nutrients.
  • Lateral nail grooves and folds anchor the sides.
  • The hyponychium is thickened skin under the free edge.
  • The nail root is the production site near the bone.
  • The eponychium, or cuticle, is a fold of stratum corneum over the nail root.
  • The lunula is the pale crescent at the base where blood vessels are obscured.
Helpful scaffold: Accessory structures are specialized tools built from the skin's surface tissue but anchored deeper in the support layer.

The skin repairs injuries through inflammatory, migratory, proliferation, and scarring phases. Aging reduces cell division, immune defense, gland activity, blood supply, elasticity, and repair speed.

Must-know points

  • Inflammatory phase: bleeding, swelling, pain, and mast cell activity.
  • Migratory phase: scab formation, macrophage cleanup, and granulation tissue formation.
  • Proliferation phase: fibroblasts produce collagen and epidermal cells migrate across granulation tissue.
  • Scarring phase: the scab dissolves, collagen networks strengthen, capillaries decline, and scar tissue forms.
  • Keloids are raised scars that extend beyond the original wound because too much collagen is produced.

Repair sequence

  • A scab stabilizes and protects the wound.
  • Macrophages remove debris and pathogens.
  • Fibroblasts and endothelial cells help create granulation tissue.
  • Fibroblasts later produce collagen fibers that replace damaged tissue.
  • Epidermal cells migrate over the temporary tissue to rebuild the epidermis.

Aging effects

  • The epidermis thins as cell division slows, increasing vulnerability to injury and infection.
  • Fewer dendritic cells weaken immune response.
  • Reduced vitamin D3 production can contribute to weaker bones and higher osteoporosis risk.
  • Reduced melanocyte activity causes paler skin and gray or white hair.
  • Reduced glandular activity causes dry, scaly skin and impairs temperature regulation.
  • Decreased blood supply slows healing and makes skin cooler and more fragile.
  • Loss of elastic fibers causes wrinkles and sagging.
  • Reduced fibroblast activity and cell turnover slow repair.
Helpful scaffold: Repair is like rebuilding a damaged wall: first stop the damage, clear debris, build temporary support, then strengthen and remodel.
